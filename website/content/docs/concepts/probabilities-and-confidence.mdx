Every Kev answer comes with numbers. They are the reason to use Kev instead of a chatbot, so it's worth understanding exactly what they mean.

## Probabilities

For every possible answer, Kev returns a probability between 0 and 1. The probabilities for one question add up to 1.

```json
"probabilities": { "billing": 0.75, "shipping": 0.15, "account": 0.10 }
```

Read this as: "if I saw many tickets like this one, about 75% of them would belong to billing."

That reading only works if the probabilities are **calibrated**, meaning that when Kev says 0.75, it's right about 75% of the time. Most models aren't. Neural networks trained the usual way tend to be **overconfident**: they say 0.99 when they're right 85% of the time.

Kev ships with a correction built in. Each checkpoint stores a single number, a **temperature** (between 2.1 and 2.4 for the released models), that softens overconfident probabilities. The temperature was fitted so that probabilities match observed accuracy on held-out data. It never changes which answer is picked; it only makes the numbers more honest.

The effect is large. On datasets Kev never trained on, the share of answers that were wrong despite a probability of at least 0.9 dropped from 6.9% to 2.6% for Kev-4B and from 8.7% to 4.0% for Kev-9B. Jev's figure on the same questions is 3.7%.

<Callout type="warn" title="Calibration is measured on benchmarks, not on your data">
  The temperature was fitted on Kev's own development data. On data that looks very different (a new language, a niche domain, very long documents), probabilities can drift. Check them on a sample of your own labeled data before relying on a threshold. [Measure on your data](#measure-on-your-own-data) shows how.
</Callout>

## Confidence

Choice and Score answers also have a `confidence` field. It's a single 0-to-1 summary of how decisive the probabilities are, so you can write one `if` statement instead of inspecting the whole distribution.

For **Choice**, confidence measures how far the top probability is above a random guess. With 3 options, a random guess is 1/3. If the top option has probability 1/3, confidence is 0. If it has probability 1, confidence is 1.

```text
confidence = (top probability − 1/options) ÷ (1 − 1/options)
```

For **Score**, confidence is higher when the probability is concentrated around one level. Mass on neighboring levels costs less than mass far away, because "medium or high" is a more coherent answer than "low or high".

**Noul** answers don't have a confidence field. The probability of yes already says it: 0.97 and 0.03 are both confident; 0.5 is a coin flip.

<Callout title="Confidence is a routing signal, not a guarantee">
  A confidence of 0.8 doesn't mean "80% chance this is right". It means the probabilities are concentrated. Use it to decide _which_ answers to automate and which to send to a person. What fraction of automated answers turn out to be right is something you measure, not something the number promises.
</Callout>

## Setting a threshold

The typical pattern is: act automatically when Kev is sure, and ask a person when it isn't.

```python
answer = response.choices["department"]
if answer.confidence >= 0.6:
    route_to(answer.choice)
else:
    send_to_triage_queue()
```

How do you pick 0.6? The honest answer is: from data. A threshold is a trade-off between **coverage** (how many decisions you automate) and **error rate** (how many of those are wrong). A higher threshold automates fewer decisions but makes fewer mistakes.

On the new-source development set, as served, Kev-4B can automate 57% of decisions and Kev-9B 45% while keeping the error rate at or below 5%. Jev can automate 70% at the same error rate. Your numbers will be different, because they depend on how hard your questions are.

## Measure on your own data

You need a few hundred examples where you know the right answer. Then:

1. Send each example to Kev and save the answer and its confidence.
2. Sort by confidence, highest first.
3. Walk down the list and track the error rate among the examples above each point.
4. Pick the lowest threshold where the error rate is still acceptable for your product.

Kev's benchmark tool does this for you and reports accuracy, calibration and coverage at a 5% error budget. Put your examples in a JSONL file in the [fine-tuning format](/docs/guides/fine-tuning#prepare-your-data) and run:

```bash
uv run python -m kev.benchmark --remote http://127.0.0.1:8009 --data my-labeled.jsonl --out runs/my-eval
```

`--remote` works against any Kev or TypeSafe-compatible endpoint, including a [Modal deployment](/docs/deploy/modal).
